The factor objective under the mandate: spending a budget of tracking error on factor exposure in French's 49 industries

Carlo Hofer. November 2026. The second note of the project Optimal versus naive diversification. Code and notebooks: github.com/ochofer/optimal-vs-naive-diversification.

The first note found that a long-only index-tracking mandate on French's 49 industries leaves small factor exposures that nobody chose. This note spends tracking error on factor exposure deliberately. Each month from 1979 to 2026 the optimiser maximises the portfolio's exposure to value, profitability, investment and momentum under the mandate, within a budget of 75, 100 or 150 basis points a year of forecast tracking error. At 100 basis points the combined exposure is 0.109 to 0.140 and the realised tracking error 19 to 50 per cent above the budget. The targeted factors contributed +26.7 to +35.5 basis points a year, statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on four of the five estimates, on a Newey-West standard error, and the six factors +37 to +47, but the residual, what the factors leave unexplained, was −31 to −37, so no path's active return is statistically significant at that level.

Terms

Benchmark
the cap-weighted portfolio of French's 49 industry portfolios.
Active weight
an industry's portfolio weight minus its benchmark weight.
Active return
the portfolio's return minus the benchmark's.
Tracking error
the standard deviation of the monthly active return, times the square root of 12. It is forecast before the month and realised after it.
Basis point
a hundredth of a percentage point.
Mandate
the rules the portfolio obeys, set out in Section 2.
Beta
the slope of an industry's return on a factor's return, estimated on the three years of daily returns before each month.
Active exposure
the portfolio's beta to a factor minus the benchmark's, i.e., the sum over industries of the active weight times the industry's beta.
Targeted factors
value, profitability, investment and momentum, each counted in the direction of its premium.
Factor objective
the sum of the portfolio's active exposures to the four targeted factors, which the optimiser maximises.
Budget
the limit on the forecast tracking error, 75, 100 or 150 basis points a year.
Path
the month-by-month sequence of portfolios built with one covariance estimate at one budget.
Bias statistic
the standard deviation of the realised active return divided by its forecast. It is 1 for an accurate forecast and above 1 when the forecast is too low.
Residual
the part of the active return the six factors do not explain, i.e., the active return minus the six factor contributions. It comes from the industries' returns beyond what their factor exposures imply.
Factor share
the share of the variance of the active return that the six factors explain.
Exposure per basis point
a path's mean exposure to the targeted factors divided by its realised tracking error in basis points a year.
Gap
the part of a month's factor objective that is lost when the same weights are valued with the betas estimated after the month.
Newey-West standard error
a standard error of a mean of monthly figures that allows for correlation between months up to six months apart (Newey and West, 1987).

1. Introduction

An enhanced index fund spends a budget of tracking error on purpose, taking factor exposures its benchmark does not hold while staying close to it (Robeco, 2024). The first note of this project (Hofer, 2026) measured what a long-only index-tracking mandate on French's 49 industries costs when nothing is spent on purpose: the portfolio closest to the benchmark realises 84 to 96 basis points a year of tracking error, and the mandate's tilt away from Coal, Oil and Utilities leaves small exposures to the common factors that nobody chose. The question this note asks is the one the holder of that mandate asks next: how much exposure to four factors a given budget of tracking error obtains, and what that exposure contributes to the return.

The factor literature documents the premia and their long-run averages (Fama and French, 2015; Carhart, 1997), and descriptions of enhanced indexing describe portfolios that take factor exposures within a tracking-error limit (Robeco, 2024). Neither shows, under a given mandate, how much exposure is obtained per basis point of tracking error when every industry outside a tilt must stay within 2 percentage points of its benchmark weight, how far the realised tracking error exceeds the budget the optimiser was held to, which factors the optimiser actually buys when all four are weighted equally, and what share of the resulting active return the factors explain when the assets are industries.

This note tests this on French's 49 industries. Each month from July 1979 to August 2026 the optimiser maximises the sum of the portfolio's active exposures to value, profitability, investment and momentum, under every rule of the mandate and a budget on the forecast tracking error of 75, 100 or 150 basis points a year, with the forecast from one of the first note's five covariance estimates. Seven expectations about the results were written before any path ran, and Table 5 lists them. An attribution then splits each path's active return into the contributions of six factors and a residual, the part the factors do not explain.

French's 49 industries are well suited to test this question, because the first note has already measured the mandate on the same months, so every figure here has a counterpart without the objective. At the same time, the industries' betas to the four factors differ by about 0.5, so a bound of 2 percentage points per industry allows exposures of 0.03 to 0.08 per factor, large enough to measure and small enough for a realistic mandate.

Empirically, the budget is spent almost in full, but unevenly across the four factors. At 100 basis points the optimiser obtains 0.109 to 0.140 of exposure to the four factors together, 74 to 85 per cent of it in profitability and investment and almost none in momentum, and the realised tracking error is 118.6 to 137.0 basis points a year on the daily estimators and 150.1 on the monthly covariance, 19 to 50 per cent above the budget. The targeted factors contributed +26.7 to +35.5 basis points a year, statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on four of the five estimates, 2.0 to 2.4 standard errors from zero, on the Newey-West standard error, and not on the monthly covariance. The residual, i.e., the part of the active return the factors do not explain, was −31 to −37 against the six factors' +37 to +47, however, so no path's active return is statistically significant at that level, which at 100 basis points would take one of about 34 to 43 basis points a year.

2. Design

The data, the benchmark and the mandate are those of the first note. French's 49 value-weighted industry portfolios and his six factors come from his library, on the files built from the CRSP database of August 2026, and the benchmark is the cap-weighted portfolio of the 49 industries. The mandate holds the portfolio long-only, each industry other than Coal, Oil and Utilities within 2 percentage points of its benchmark weight, one-way turnover at most 2 per cent a month, those three at no more than half their benchmark weight, tobacco and weapons excluded, and the beta to the benchmark at one.

The objective replaces the first note's. Instead of the smallest forecast tracking error, the optimiser seeks the largest factor objective, the sum of the portfolio's active exposures to value, profitability, investment and momentum, each counted in the direction of its premium and weighted equally. An active exposure is the sum over industries of the active weight times the industry's beta to the factor, with each industry's betas estimated on its daily returns over the three years before the month. The market is left out because the mandate already holds the portfolio's beta at one, and size is not targeted. Every rule of the mandate still applies, and the forecast tracking error from the covariance in use must stay at or below the budget. In a month when even the portfolio closest to the benchmark exceeds the budget, the optimiser takes that portfolio, and the month counts as one in which the budget was not met.

Nineteen paths run over the 566 months, fifteen main paths and four more at 100 basis points. The main paths are the five covariance estimates of the first note at each of the three budgets: the sample covariance, Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage (Ledoit and Wolf, 2004), a six-factor model and a principal-component model on three years of daily returns, and the sample covariance of 120 monthly returns. The four more are the robust portfolio, whose budget holds under all five covariances at once, RiskMetrics, and the sample covariance targeting value alone and momentum alone. Each path is judged by its realised tracking error against the budget, its bias statistic, its active return with its standard error, its turnover and trading cost, and the exposures it holds.

The attribution splits each month's active return into six contributions, each the active exposure to a factor times the factor's return in the month, and a residual. Two measures describe how the budget is spent, the exposure per basis point and the gap. The exposure per basis point divides a path's mean exposure by its realised tracking error. The gap measures how much of the exposure chosen with the betas known before the month is lost when the same weights are valued with the betas estimated after it. Seven expectations were written into the repository before any path ran, and Table 5 states each with its verdict, met or not met. E1 and E2 were set on 9 October 2026, after the static run of notebook 14 on the same data and before any path ran. The notebooks mark the comparisons added after the results were known.

3. Results

3.1 The exposures obtained

Table 1 reports the nineteen paths and Table 2 the exposures they hold. At 100 basis points the budget binds, within one basis point, in 96.6 to 97.3 per cent of the months the mandate alone can meet on the four daily estimators (expectation E1, met). The mandate alone cannot meet the budget in 22 to 43 of the 566 months, nearly all in 1979 to 1989, when Oil held 15 per cent of the benchmark and the tilt alone produced more than 100 basis points of forecast tracking error. All four targeted exposures are positive on every estimator, and the mean exposure rises with the budget on every estimator (E2, met). Against the exposures the mandate leaves behind on its own, between -0.015 and -0.004 on the four factors, the factor objective reverses the sign of all four and makes them three to five times as large.

The exposure is spread unevenly across the four factors. Per basis point of realised tracking error the five main paths at 100 basis points obtain 0.00092 to 0.00096 of exposure to the four together, but profitability and investment account for 74 to 85 per cent of it, value for most of the rest and momentum for almost none, 0.00001 to 0.00007. The four are weighted equally in the objective, and the optimiser buys most of the two whose industry betas it can raise at the least forecast tracking error within the bounds, and least of momentum, whose industry betas are the smallest and change fastest. An equal weighting in the objective therefore does not produce equal exposures. Targeting a single factor shows the trade-off: the value-only path obtains 0.00060 of value per basis point and loses 0.00030 of investment, and the momentum-only path obtains 0.00032 of momentum and loses enough value and investment that its exposure to the four together is 0.00002 per basis point, against 0.00096 for the four-factor path on the same estimate.

3.2 The tracking error realised

The realised tracking error exceeds the budget on every path. At 100 basis points the ratio of the realised tracking error to the budget is 1.19 to 1.37 on the daily estimators and 1.50 on the monthly covariance. At 150 it is 1.14 to 1.25 and 1.28, and at 75 it is 1.31 to 1.50 and 1.71. Expectation E3 asked for 1.2 to 1.6 on the daily estimators at every budget and is not met, because three of the twelve daily ratios fall below that range, so the forecast underestimated the tracking error by less than expected. The direction is the first note's, since an optimiser picks the weights for which the forecast is lowest relative to the truth (Michaud, 1989). The size is smaller, because a portfolio held at a budget is less of an in-sample minimum than the portfolio with the smallest forecast: the bias statistics at 100 basis points are 1.16 to 1.35 on the daily estimators, against the first note's 1.22 to 1.39. The robust portfolio is again the best calibrated, at 1.08, and the monthly covariance the worst at 100 basis points, at 1.45.

The choice of estimator matters for the realised tracking error and little for the exposure obtained. At 100 basis points the realised tracking errors of the four daily estimators span 18.4 basis points on a level of about 130, in the first note's order, the sample covariance and shrinkage lowest and the two structured models highest, while their exposure per basis point lies within 4.5 per cent of its mean (E7, met). The turnover cap binds in 378 to 518 of the 566 months, the portfolios turn over 1.78 to 2.00 per cent a month one way against the benchmark's 0.39, and at 50 basis points per unit of turnover the trading costs 10.7 to 12.0 basis points a year.

3.3 The active return, and where it came from

Table 3 reports the attribution. At 100 basis points the gross active return is +6.0 to +15.5 basis points a year on standard errors of 17.3 to 21.9. No path's active return reaches 1.4 standard errors, so none is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level, and net of trading costs the return at 100 basis points is -6.0 to +4.8 (E5: the gross return is positive on all five estimates, as expected, and trading costs more than the 10 basis points expected). The four targeted factors contributed +26.7 to +35.5 basis points a year at 100 and +49.9 to +58.9 at 150, statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on the Newey-West standard error on nine of those ten paths, all but the monthly covariance's at 100, and +5.7 to +16.6 at 75, which is not statistically significant on any of the five, where it would need about 21 to 31 basis points a year to be. These contributions are the exposures times the factors' realised returns over these 47 years, and the study estimates no premium.

The attribution's main finding is that the residual offsets most of the six factors' contribution. The residual is negative on every one of the nineteen paths, -10 to -47 basis points a year, and at 100 basis points it offsets 31 to 37 of the +37 to +47 that the six factors contribute together. The two parts move against each other because the same active weights carry both: in months when the targeted factors returned more, the industries held to obtain the exposures returned less than their factor exposures imply. As a result the six factors explain 14 to 28 per cent of the variance of the active return at 100 and 150 basis points, where at least 30 per cent was expected (E4, not met in this clause), close to the 14 to 25 per cent of the mandate alone. Table 4 shows how: the factors' own variance is 34 to 52 per cent of the active return's, the residual's 72 to 86 per cent, and their covariance takes 18 to 26 per cent off the sum. By decade, at 100 basis points, the targeted factors contributed most in the 1990s, +70 to +93 basis points a year, and the residual was most negative in the same decade, -104 to -143, so the decade's active return lies between -17 and +14.

The two factors outside the objective are not at zero either. The beta constraint holds the portfolio's beta to the benchmark, the cap-weighted 49 industries, at one, and the industries the optimiser buys move more with French's market factor than those it sells. The active exposure to the market factor is therefore 0.012 to 0.015 at 100 basis points on the daily estimators, and it contributed +9.8 to +11.7 basis points a year. Size contributed +1.2 to +2.8.

3.4 The exposure lost when the betas move

Table 4 also reports the gap. At 100 basis points, valuing the weights with the betas estimated after the month reduces the exposure chosen before it by about 4 per cent, 0.039 to 0.044 per unit of exposure, and by more at 75 basis points, 0.054 to 0.076, where the exposure is smaller and the betas move as much. The largest monthly gaps fall in October 1990, July 2005, September 1981 and October 1987, months in which a large shock entered or left the three-year window the betas are estimated on. Momentum alone has the higher turnover, 1.94 per cent a month against value alone's 1.87, and its turnover cap binds more often, in 478 months against 408, but its gap in beta units is smaller, 0.00185 against 0.00235 (E6, not met in this clause). Per unit of exposure held the order is the expected one, 0.047 against 0.032, because the momentum-only path holds about half as much exposure.

The factor share is one minus the variance of the residual over the variance of the active return. The three shares are the factor total's own variance, the residual's own variance and twice their covariance, each over the variance of the active return, and they sum to one. The gap of a month is the factor objective chosen with the betas estimated before the month minus the value the same active weights carry with the betas estimated after it. The mean absolute gap is over the 565 months that have a following month, and the gap per unit divides it by the path's mean factor objective.

4. Discussion

The natural comparison is with the first note's mandate on the same months. There the tracking error, 84 to 96 basis points a year, came from rules not chosen for return, and the exposures the tilt left behind contributed -9 to -13 basis points a year while the residual contributed +12 to +20. Here the tracking error is spent deliberately, and the estimates indicate that the optimiser obtains the exposures the arithmetic implies: a budget of 100 basis points yields 0.109 to 0.140 of exposure, and the four targeted factors contribute +26.7 to +35.5 basis points a year. The comparison also shows what the factor objective does not change. The residual remains the larger part of the variance of the active return and moves against the factor part, so the active return, +6 to +16 basis points a year gross at 100 on standard errors of 17 to 22, is not statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on any path, as the mandate's was not.

For the holder of such a mandate, the estimates therefore suggest three things. First, when the budget is spent on equally weighted factors, the optimiser obtains mostly the factors whose industry betas it can raise at little tracking error, here profitability and investment, so an equal weighting in the objective does not mean equal exposures in the portfolio. Second, the realised tracking error of a portfolio held at a budget by its own forecast exceeds the budget, by 14 to 50 per cent on the daily estimators and 28 to 71 per cent on the monthly covariance, so a budget written on the forecast limits a smaller tracking error than the one realised. Third, the targeted factors contributed what the sample's factor returns imply, but the active return a client receives is that contribution net of the residual, and over these 47 years it is not statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on any path.

At the same time, the design has limits. First, the assets are industry portfolios, so each basis point of tracking error yields about 0.001 of exposure, and at 150 basis points the mandate's bounds, and not the budget, limit the exposure in 104 to 201 of the 566 months. Whether a stock-level mandate obtains more exposure per basis point is a question these data cannot answer. Second, the betas come from three years of daily returns, and both the exposure obtained and the gap depend on that window. Third, the factor share depends on its definition: as one minus the residual's share, the definition of the first note, it is 0.14 to 0.28, while the factors' own variance alone is 0.34 to 0.52 of the active return's, and Table 4 reports both. Finally, the targeted factors' contribution is their realised returns over these 566 months times the exposures, so whether its sign would hold over another sample is a question these data cannot answer.

Against this evidence, the claim is a narrow one. On French's 49 industries, under the first note's mandate, a budget of forecast tracking error spent on four factors obtained positive exposure to each of them, mostly in profitability and investment, at a realised tracking error 14 to 71 per cent above the budget. The targeted factors' contributions were positive on every main path and statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on the Newey-West standard error on nine of the ten main paths at 100 and 150 basis points, the residual offset most of the six factors' contribution, and no path's active return is statistically significant at that level.
