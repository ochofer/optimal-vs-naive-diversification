Optimal versus naive diversification: a replication, and what a tracking-error mandate changes

Carlo Hofer. November 2026. Code and notebooks: github.com/ochofer/optimal-vs-naive-diversification.

DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009) find that none of the optimised portfolios they test is consistently better than the naive 1/N rule out of sample. I replicate their study and ask their question of an index fund, i.e., whether the choice of covariance estimator still matters when a long-only portfolio must track its benchmark under a mandate. The replication reproduces 29 of the 30 Sharpe ratios it tests, and the paper's conclusion holds in the 261 months since its sample. On Ken French's 49 industries from 1979 to 2026, under a mandate that halves the weight of Coal, Oil and Utilities, the estimator still matters, by about a tenth of the tracking error (7 to 9 of 85 to 93 basis points a year), and every estimator's forecast underestimates the realised tracking error. The tilt has no cost in return that is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level.

Terms

1/N
the rule that holds every asset at the same weight and rebalances to it each month.
Excess return
a return minus the one-month Treasury bill rate.
Sharpe ratio
the mean monthly excess return divided by its standard deviation.
Turnover
the sum of the absolute weight changes at a rebalance. One-way turnover is half of it, the share of the portfolio sold.
Estimation window
the months of returns a rule sees when it forms its weights, 120 in the paper.
Vintage
the version of French's files, named by the CRSP database they were built from.
Jobson-Korkie test
the test of whether two Sharpe ratios differ, with Memmel's correction, as the paper uses it.
Band
the distance from a published figure within which a replicated figure counts as a match, its width fixed before the code ran.
Benchmark
the cap-weighted portfolio of the 49 industries that the fund tracks.
Active return
the portfolio's return minus the benchmark's.
Tracking error
the standard deviation of the monthly active return, times the square root of 12. It is forecast before the month and realised after it.
Tilt
holding Coal, Oil and Utilities at no more than half their benchmark weight.
Mandate
the full set of rules the fund's portfolio obeys, set out in Section 2.
Covariance estimate
the table of the variances and covariances of the assets' returns, from which the optimiser forecasts tracking error.
Shrinkage
pulling an estimate towards a simpler target.
Structured models
the factor model and the principal-component model, which build the covariance from five or six common drivers plus each asset's own variance.
Principal component
a weighted combination of the assets' returns that captures the largest variance the earlier components leave.
Attribution
the split of each month's active return into the contributions of six factors and a residual, the part the factors do not explain.
Bias statistic
the standard deviation of the realised active return divided by its forecast. It is 1 for an accurate forecast and above 1 when the forecast is too low.
Basis point
a hundredth of a percentage point.
Paired variance test
the test of Ledoit and Wolf (2011) of whether two portfolios, measured on the same months, have the same tracking error.
Holm's correction
an adjustment of p-values for the number of comparisons read together (Holm, 1979).
Newey-West standard error
a standard error of a mean of monthly figures that allows for correlation between months up to six months apart (Newey and West, 1987).

1. Introduction

Whether an optimised portfolio beats the naive 1/N rule is an old question in portfolio construction, and DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009) gave the answer that has become the reference point. On seven datasets, none of fourteen optimising rules beat 1/N consistently out of sample, because the error in means and covariances estimated from 120 months of returns costs more than the optimisation gains (Michaud, 1989; Jagannathan and Ma, 2003). An index fund faces a narrower version of the question. Its mandate fixes most of the portfolio, and the one estimate its optimiser needs is a covariance matrix that forecasts tracking error. The question it poses is therefore whether the choice of covariance estimator still matters once the mandate's constraints bind.

Dom, Howard, Jansen and Lohre (2025) ask this question of the minimum-variance portfolio of the 500 largest US stocks. They find that shrinkage and time-varying covariance estimators beat the sample covariance when the portfolio is unconstrained, that most of this advantage disappears once the portfolio is long-only with a cap on each stock's weight and a penalty on trading, and that time variation still lowers volatility. Whether their result carries over to an index fund is open, for two reasons, the kind of constraint and the size of what is minimised. First, a mandate constrains the portfolio in a different way, holding each position close to its benchmark weight and capping trading. Second, the quantity it minimises, the variance of the portfolio's return relative to the benchmark, is more than a hundred times smaller than the variance of the market, so the same estimation error can weigh more or less.

This note does two things. First, it replicates DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009) on the four datasets from French's library for which the paper prints tables, against bands whose widths were fixed before the code ran, and extends their comparison to the 261 months since their sample. Second, it asks their question of a tracking-error mandate on French's 49 industry portfolios. Each month from July 1979 to August 2026, an optimiser finds the long-only portfolio with the smallest forecast tracking error under the mandate, using one of seven covariance estimates, and the tracking error the portfolio then realises is the outcome. The design holds no view on expected returns, so it measures what the mandate costs in tracking error and in realised return and how much the estimator matters, and it says nothing about what a return forecast would add.

French's 49 industries are well suited to test this question, for three reasons. First, the data are public and every figure here is computed on a recorded version of French's files, so every figure can be rebuilt. Second, the industries' weights in the market can be rebuilt from French's own firm counts and firm sizes, and the rebuilt benchmark tracks his market return within 47 basis points a year, so the cost of the mandate is measured against a known reference. Third, the industries differ in their exposure to the common factors, so halving three of them creates factor exposures large enough to measure and small enough, about 0.01 to 0.02, to be a realistic side effect of a sustainability mandate. The tracking error the mandate reaches, about 1 per cent a year, is the ex-ante limit of Robeco's Global Developed Enhanced Index Equities strategy (Robeco, 2024, p. 19).

Empirically, the replication reproduces 29 of the 30 Sharpe ratios and all 30 turnover figures it tests, and in the 261 months since the paper no optimising rule's Sharpe ratio exceeds 1/N's by a margin that is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level. Under the mandate the estimator still matters, though by about a tenth of the tracking error. The four estimators built from daily returns realise 84.7 to 93.4 basis points a year and the sample covariance of monthly returns 95.8. The two structured models realise 7 to 9 basis points more than the sample covariance and shrinkage, each difference statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on a paired test after Holm's correction across nine comparisons. Every estimator's forecast underestimates the realised tracking error, the realised active return being 22 to 61 per cent more volatile than forecast, and for the daily estimators most of the underestimate comes from the years before 2000. The tilt is associated with an active return of +2 to +7 basis points a year, which is not statistically significant at the 5 per cent level, and the data rule out a cost of more than about 21 basis points a year.

2. Data and design

All data come from Ken French's library and are downloaded when the code runs. The repository stores only the tables the code writes, together with each file's download date and hash, and every figure here is computed on the files French built from the CRSP database of August 2026. Returns are monthly excess returns. The extension also uses the daily returns of the 49 industries and of the factors.

The replication runs the paper's nine rules on its four datasets: Industry (ten industries and the market), MKT/SMB/HML (the three Fama-French factors), FF-1-factor (20 size and book-to-market portfolios and the market) and FF-4-factor (the same 20 portfolios with four factors). Each month a rule forms its weights from the previous 120 months and holds them for one month, over the paper's sample from July 1963 to November 2004, which gives 377 out-of-sample months. The rules are 1/N, sample mean-variance, mean-variance with the means shrunk towards a common value (Bayes-Stein, Jorion, 1986), minimum variance, the value-weighted market, the versions of mean-variance, Bayes-Stein and minimum variance that allow no negative weight, and minimum variance with every weight at least 1/(2N). The match is judged against three bands: each Sharpe ratio within 0.03 of the published value, the top three rules of each dataset in the published order, and each turnover figure within 25 per cent of the published one. The Sharpe band applies only where the paper's own turnover for the rule is below 100 times that of 1/N, because above that level ten basis points of noise added to every return move the rule's Sharpe ratio by 0.12 to 0.25. This leaves 30 tested Sharpe ratios and 30 tested turnover figures. That restriction of the Sharpe ratio band was set on 13 September 2026, after notebook 02 had run, on that test. On all 36 out-of-sample Sharpe ratios, 31 are within 0.03. The paper's analytical result (its Proposition 1) and its simulation (its Table 6) are rerun as well, and the nine rules are then run on every month to August 2026. A rule's Sharpe ratio is compared with 1/N's using the Jobson-Korkie test with Memmel's correction, two-sided, as in the paper (Jobson and Korkie, 1981; Memmel, 2003).

The extension's benchmark is the cap-weighted portfolio of the 49 industries, each industry weighted by its number of firms times its average firm size from French's files. Over the 686 months from July 1969 it tracks French's market return within 46.7 basis points a year, with a correlation of 0.9996. Each month from July 1979 to August 2026, 566 months, an optimiser chooses the long-only weights with the smallest forecast tracking error against this benchmark, starting from the previous month's weights as the month's returns have moved them. The mandate holds each industry other than Coal, Oil and Utilities within 2 percentage points of its benchmark weight, allows one-way turnover of at most 2 per cent a month (relaxed only in a month when the benchmark itself moves more than that), holds those three at no more than half their benchmark weight, excludes tobacco and weapons, and sets the portfolio's beta to the benchmark at one. To separate the cost of the tilt from the rest of the mandate, the same optimiser also runs with the tilt alone, without the exclusions and the beta constraint.

Seven covariance estimates are compared, each built only from data before the month: four from three years of daily returns, the sample covariance of 120 monthly returns, a robust portfolio and RiskMetrics. The four daily estimates come first. The first is the sample covariance. The second is Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage, which pulls the sample covariance towards a simpler target by an amount the data choose (Ledoit and Wolf, 2004). The third is a six-factor model, which builds the covariance from each industry's exposures to the market, size, value, profitability, investment and momentum factors plus its own variance. The fourth is a principal-component model, which does the same with the benchmark and the four largest statistical components of what the benchmark leaves. The fifth is the sample covariance of the last 120 monthly returns, as in the paper. The sixth is a robust portfolio, which minimises the largest forecast across these five. The seventh is RiskMetrics, an exponentially weighted covariance of five years of daily returns with a half-life of 252 trading days, close to the decay of 0.99734 in Dom, Howard, Jansen and Lohre (2025), about 260 days.

Each path, i.e., the month-by-month sequence of portfolios built with one estimate, is judged by its realised tracking error, its mean forecast, its bias statistic, its active return, its turnover and its trading cost at 50 basis points per unit of turnover. The realised tracking errors of two paths are compared with the paired variance test, in its bootstrap form with blocks of twelve months, on the months the two paths share, and with Holm's correction where more than one comparison is read together. The standard error of a mean active return is the realised tracking error divided by the square root of the 47 years. Finally, each month's active return is split into the contributions of the six factors and a residual: an industry's exposures to the factors come from a regression of its daily returns on the factors over the previous three years, and the portfolio's active exposure to a factor is the sum of its active weights times those exposures. Each expectation was written into the repository before the code that tests it ran. Five analyses were added after the monthly results were known: the paired tests of Table 5, the measurement on daily returns, whose settings were public before its own results, the split of the bias statistic at 2000, the standard error from whole years for the daily tests and the Newey-West standard errors of the factor attribution. The notebooks mark each one.

3. Results

3.1 The replication

Table 1 reports the out-of-sample Sharpe ratios beside the paper's. Of the 30 tested cells, 29 are within the band of 0.03 and 27 within 0.01, and the 1/N cells, which involve no estimation, are within 0.001 of the paper on three of the four datasets. The one miss is minimum variance on FF-4-factor, 0.033 against the paper's -0.018. Noise does not account for it, since ten basis points of noise move the cell over a range of 0.08 that does not reach the published value, and the in-sample row points instead to a revision of French's size and book-to-market portfolios. The ranking of the top three rules holds on two datasets and fails narrowly on the other two, where the rules that change places have published Sharpe ratios 0.002 to 0.007 apart, closer than a band of 0.03 can resolve. The six differences between a constrained rule and 1/N that the paper reports as significant at the 5 per cent level all reproduce on its one-sided test.

Table 2 reports turnover, and all 30 tested cells are within 25 per cent of the published figures. The paper's analytical result and its simulation also reproduce: the estimation window at which mean-variance starts to beat 1/N on average matches all 13 values the paper states, and 77 of the 81 cells of its simulation are within 0.03. In two further simulated markets, one with fat-tailed returns and one with volatility that clusters over time, the comparison with 1/N is essentially unchanged.

3.3 The estimators under the mandate

Without constraints the estimator matters, as Dom, Howard, Jansen and Lohre (2025) find. The unconstrained minimum-variance portfolio built from the sample covariance of 120 monthly returns realises 14.1 per cent volatility a year, against 12.1 to 12.8 per cent for shrinkage and the two structured models on the same data. On three years of daily returns, by contrast, the sample covariance is as good as any (11.9 per cent against 11.9 to 13.5), and once the portfolio is long-only every estimator at either frequency lands within 0.2 points of the others, at 12.1 to 12.3 per cent.

Table 4 reports the mandate. Without the tilt, the weight bound and the turnover cap keep the portfolio within 6.5 basis points a year of the benchmark, so almost all of the mandate's tracking error comes from the tilt. Under the full mandate the daily sample covariance realises 84.7 basis points a year, shrinkage 84.8, RiskMetrics 84.3, the robust portfolio 87.4, the principal-component model 91.5, the factor model 93.4 and the monthly sample covariance 95.8. The tilt alone accounts for 94 to 95 per cent of these levels, and the exclusions and the beta constraint add 4 to 5 basis points.

Table 5 reports the paired tests, which indicate that the choice of estimator matters by about a tenth of the tracking error. Against the daily sample covariance, the principal-component model realises 6.8 basis points more, with a 95 per cent interval of 2.2 to 11.5, and the factor model 8.8 more, with an interval of 4.7 to 12.9. Each difference is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on the paired test after Holm's correction across the nine comparisons of panels A and B, and against shrinkage the two differences are 8.6 and 6.7. Shrinkage itself differs from the sample covariance by 0.2 basis points, which is not statistically significant, and the data rule out a difference of more than about half a basis point: on three years of daily returns the two are almost the same estimate, with a shrinkage intensity, i.e., the weight on the simpler target, of 0.012. The 7 to 9 basis points the two structured models add are therefore plausibly the cost of assuming that industries share nothing beyond their common drivers. The monthly sample covariance realises 11.1 basis points more than the daily one, which is not statistically significant on monthly returns, where the test has too little power to detect a difference of that size: it would detect one of 27.7 four times in five.

RiskMetrics, the one estimator with time variation, realises 0.4 basis points less than the sample covariance (interval -2.3 to +1.5), which is not statistically significant at the 5 per cent level. With 566 months the test has too little power to detect a gain, i.e., lower risk, of the size Dom, Howard, Jansen and Lohre (2025) report, 2.7 per cent of the level or 2.28 basis points here: it would find one statistically significant only about two times in three. Measured on the 11,888 trading days, the comparison is far more precise, the standard error of the difference falling from 0.91 to 0.17 basis points. On days RiskMetrics realises 0.59 basis points a year less than the sample covariance on three years of data (interval -0.95 to -0.23), which is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level after Holm's correction across six comparisons, and 0.37 less than the sample covariance on the same five years (interval -0.88 to +0.14), which is not. In both cases the data rule out a gain as large as theirs, 2.01 and 2.00 basis points at these levels. The two structured models stay above the sample covariance on days, by 3.4 and 4.1 basis points, each statistically significant at the 5 per cent level after Holm's correction. On a standard error from whole years of trading days, added after these results were known, the differences of the two structured models and of the monthly sample covariance stay statistically significant at the 5 per cent level after Holm's correction, and those of RiskMetrics, shrinkage and the robust portfolio do not. The daily tracking errors, 68.9 to 87.2 basis points a year, sit below those of Table 4 because each path's variance on months is 1.21 to 1.46 times its variance on days.

Why the structured models lose. The two structured models assume uncorrelated residuals, i.e., that the part of each industry's return the factors leave unexplained moves independently of every other industry's. Under the mandate this assumption matters, because the six factors explain only 14 to 25 per cent of the active return's variance. Two further tests, run after these results, ask whether the 7 to 9 basis points a year the two models add are the cost of that assumption, set against the estimation error it saves.

In the first, the two models get back a share of the residual covariance between industries, from none to all of it. With all of it, both become the sample covariance. On daily returns the six-factor model's tracking error falls from 78.6 basis points a year with none to 75.7 with a quarter, 74.4 with half and 74.0 with three quarters, against the sample covariance's 74.5. The principal-component model's falls from 77.9 to 75.5, 74.4 and 74.1. On monthly returns both fall at every step, to the sample covariance's 84.7.

In the second, the four daily estimators are built from 3, 6 and 12 months of daily returns as well as three years. The shorter the window, the fewer returns there are per number to estimate: the window holds about 2.5, 5 and 10 returns for each of the covariance matrix's 1,225 numbers, against 30 with three years. On daily returns, the structured models' excess over the sample covariance falls from 4.1 and 3.4 basis points a year at three years to 1.9 and 1.5 at one year and about zero at six months, and at three months they realise 2.2 and 2.3 less than the sample covariance. Ledoit-Wolf's gain over the sample covariance grows from 0.15 to 2.2 basis points a year, and its median shrinkage intensity from 0.012 to 0.097. The sample covariance and Ledoit-Wolf realise more tracking error on every shorter window, and at three months every estimator trades at the turnover cap.

The two tests point the same way. With three years of daily returns the sample covariance is estimated precisely, so the structured models' assumption costs more than the estimation error it saves, and the cost disappears once the residual covariance is let back in. With less data the balance is inverted, in line with the trade-off Dom, Howard, Jansen and Lohre (2025) describe for the exact factor model.

The difference is the first tracking error minus the second, and the interval is its 95 per cent interval. At blocks of 24 months the principal-component model's two differences have a Holm value of 0.07.

3.4 The forecasts

Every estimator's forecast underestimates the realised tracking error. The bias statistic is 1.22 to 1.39 for the four daily estimators, 1.61 for the monthly sample covariance and 1.09 for the robust portfolio, whose forecast is the highest of the five by construction. The direction is what optimisation predicts: an optimiser that minimises a forecast picks the weights for which the forecast is lowest relative to the truth (Michaud, 1989), and the same estimators' forecasts for the individual industries, where no optimiser chooses, carry a bias statistic of 1.10. For the daily estimators the underestimate is concentrated before 2000. Their bias statistics are 1.62 to 1.90 in 1979 to 1989, when the oil shock fell on a portfolio tilted away from Oil, which held 15 per cent of the benchmark in 1979, and over the two periods 1.45 to 1.68 before 2000 against 1.01 to 1.12 from it. Each difference between the two periods is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on its block-bootstrap band, and none of the values from 2000 is statistically significantly different from 1. The monthly sample covariance underestimates in both periods, at 1.59 before 2000 and 1.63 from it. Scaling each month's forecast by the same path's ratio of realised to forecast tracking error over the previous 60 months brings the daily estimators to 1.11 to 1.17 and the monthly covariance to 1.12.

3.5 The mandate's return, and where it came from

The mandate's return is in the last columns of Table 4. The tilt alone is associated with an active return of +1.8 to +6.9 basis points a year across the seven estimates, against standard errors of 11.6 to 13.2, which is not statistically significant at the 5 per cent level, and the data rule out a cost of more than about 21 basis points a year. The full mandate is associated with -1.0 to -6.9 basis points a year, against standard errors of 12.3 to 13.9, which is not statistically significant either, the data ruling out a cost of more than about 32. Measured on the same months, the exclusions and the beta constraint therefore cost 7.9 to 10.4 basis points a year against the tilt alone, with Newey-West standard errors of 3.4 to 4.7, a difference that is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level under six of the seven estimates. The mandate's portfolios turn over 0.86 to 1.54 per cent a month one way against the benchmark's 0.39, and at 50 basis points per unit of turnover they pay 5.1 to 9.2 basis points a year in trading against an index fund's 2.3.

Table 6 splits the active return into the contributions of the six factors. The beta constraint holds: the active exposure to the market is between -0.005 and +0.001 on every path. The tilt leaves five other exposures, with the same signs under every estimate. Under the mandate the portfolio holds +0.010 to +0.018 of size relative to the benchmark, -0.010 to -0.015 of value, -0.008 to -0.012 of profitability, -0.005 to -0.008 of investment and -0.004 to -0.005 of momentum. The signs follow from what the tilt removes, since Oil and Utilities behave like large, cheap, profitable firms that invest little (e.g., their exposures to value are +0.27 and +0.35, against the benchmark's 0.00). The exposures are small because the weight bound is: 2 percentage points per industry, times factor exposures that differ by about 0.5 across industries, gives about 0.01.

The six factors explain 14 to 28 per cent of the variance of the active return across the fourteen paths, 14 to 25 under the mandate, so most of the tracking error comes from the residual, the part of the industries' returns that the factors do not explain and that the factor model assumes away. The attribution splits the tilt's near-zero return into two parts of opposite sign, neither statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on a Newey-West standard error. Under the tilt alone the factor contributions sum to -9.3 to -13.1 basis points a year, because the portfolio was short of value, profitability, investment and momentum, which all had positive mean returns over these years. On its Newey-West standard error of 6.6 to 7.5, the sum is not statistically significant at the 5 per cent level under any of the seven estimates. The residual, the part of the active return the factors do not explain, adds +12.2 to +19.7, which is not statistically significant under any estimate, since it would need about 18 to 24 basis points a year to be: the industries bought in place of Oil and Utilities returned more than Oil and Utilities did over these 47 years, which is the history of three industries. Under the mandate, on the sample covariance's path, the factor part was -61 basis points a year in 1979 to 1989, when the tilt was largest, and within 13 of zero in every decade since.

4. Discussion

The natural comparison is with the two papers the design rests on. On DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009), the replication indicates that their tables reproduce on a 2026 version of French's data, with the misses where the size and book-to-market file was revised and where a rule is itself fragile, and that their conclusion holds on the 261 months since: no optimising rule beats 1/N by a margin that is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level, and minimum variance, the rule that came closest in their period, fell with the premia it loaded on.

On Dom, Howard, Jansen and Lohre (2025), the extension indicates that their finding carries to a tracking-error mandate in part. Their finding that constraints shrink the differences between estimators holds here, although the mandate does not remove them: the two structured models still realise 7 to 9 basis points a year more than the sample covariance and shrinkage. Their finding on time variation is not established here under the mandate. On monthly returns the test has too little power to detect a gain of their size. On daily returns RiskMetrics realises 0.59 basis points a year less than the sample covariance on three years, which is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level on the study's test and is not, after Holm's correction, on a standard error from whole years, and 0.37 less on the same five years, the paper's own comparison, which is not statistically significant. In both comparisons the data rule out a gain as large as theirs. The minimum-variance portfolios on daily returns show a smaller gain from time variation: long-only, RiskMetrics realises 0.37 and 0.20 percentage points less volatility than the sample covariance on five and on three years of data, each difference statistically significant at the 5 per cent level, where their long-only gain would be 0.94 and 0.93 points at these levels.

For the holder of such a mandate, the estimates therefore suggest three things. First, among estimators built from three years of daily returns, the choice is worth 7 to 9 basis points a year of tracking error, about a tenth of the total, and the simplest of them, the sample covariance and shrinkage, do best. Second, the forecast of each of them underestimates the realised tracking error, by 22 to 39 per cent for the daily estimators over 1979 to 2026 and by 61 per cent for the monthly sample covariance, and scaling the forecast by the path's own recent history removes most of the underestimate. Third, the tilt's cost in return is not statistically significant at the 5 per cent level, the data ruling out a cost of more than about 21 basis points a year, but the tilt leaves factor exposures that nobody chose and that nothing in the mandate rewards: over these 47 years they contributed -9 to -13 basis points a year and the industries held in their place returned more, neither statistically significant at the 5 per cent level, a combination that need not recur.

At the same time, the design has limits. First, the assets are industry portfolios, so the findings are about 49 groups of firms. A stock-level mandate holds hundreds of positions under a much tighter bound, and whether the estimator matters by a tenth there is a question these data cannot answer. Second, the design holds no view on expected returns, so what a return forecast would add to the mandate is outside it. Third, each factor exposure of about 0.01 is a sum of estimated exposures times small active weights, so the exposures are read as means over 566 months and not month by month. Finally, French revises his files, so a rerun on a later version would reproduce the comparisons and not every digit.

Against this evidence, the claim is a narrow one. On French's 49 industries, under a mandate that holds each industry outside the tilt within 2 percentage points of its benchmark weight, trades at most 2 per cent a month and halves three industries, the covariance estimator still matters, by about a tenth of the tracking error. Every estimator's forecast underestimates the realised tracking error, and the tilt's cost in return is not statistically significant at the 5 per cent level.
