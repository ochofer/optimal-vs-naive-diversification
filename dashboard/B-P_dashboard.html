Optimal versus naive diversification: the results

Four covariance estimators under an enhanced-index mandate on Kenneth R. French's 49 industries: the tracking error they forecast and realised, what the tilt cost in tracking error, what a budget of tracking error buys in factor exposure, and a replication of DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009). Every figure is a simulation drawn from the tables committed with the code.

Sharpe ratios inside the band of 0.03 fixed before the code, four datasets
  • Under the mandate the four covariance estimators built from daily returns realise 84.7 to 93.4 basis points a year of tracking error and the monthly sample covariance 95.8, so the estimator still matters, by about a tenth.
  • Every estimator under-forecasts its own tracking error, by 22 to 61 per cent (bias statistics 1.22 to 1.61). Calibrating each forecast on the path's own previous 60 months brings the daily estimators to 1.11 to 1.17. The finding is applied in a real-money portfolio: the rules-based portfolio reports the calibration of its own risk forecast by the same measure, and its Method view explains why its split is set by a risk limit and not by an optimiser.
  • The tilt alone is associated with an active return of +2 to +7 basis points a year against a standard error of 12 to 13. Under the mandate the active return is −6.9 to −1.0 basis points a year before costs, within one standard error of zero on every path.
  • The replication of DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009) reproduces 29 of the 30 Sharpe ratios that carry a band of 0.03 and 30 of 30 turnover figures within 25 per cent. In the 261 months since the paper, none of the seven optimising rules earns a higher Sharpe ratio than 1/N at the 5 per cent level on any of the four datasets. The one cell of the 32 that does is the value-weighted market on MKT/SMB/HML, which is not an optimising rule.
  • Version 2, module A, spends a budget of forecast tracking error on the factor objective under the same mandate. At 100 basis points the budget buys exposures of 0.109 to 0.140 in beta units to value, profitability, investment and momentum together, at a realised tracking error of 118.6 to 137.0 basis points a year on the daily estimators. The four targeted factors contributed +26.7 to +35.5 basis points a year on standard errors of 11 to 17, the industries' own returns took back 31 to 37 of the factor part's +37 to +47, and no path's active return is beyond two standard errors of zero. Of the seven expectations written before the run, three are met and four are not, each stated with its arithmetic in the Factor objective view.

Whether the paper's Sharpe ratios and turnover figures reproduce, and how the rules have done since.

The replication: DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009) asked whether optimised portfolios beat 1/N out of sample and found that none of their optimising rules did so reliably. Their Tables 3 and 5 are rebuilt here on four of the five datasets the paper takes from French's library, against bands fixed before the code ran, and the comparison is run forward to August 2026.

The data, what is known at each date, the costs, what the study cannot show, and the terms.

Method

Universe and period. The extension runs on Kenneth R. French's 49 value-weighted industry portfolios and his six factors, downloaded at run time, vintage 202608. The benchmark is the cap-weighted combination of the 49, rebuilt from French's firm counts and average sizes. Every path runs over the 566 months from July 1979 to August 2026. The replication uses four of the five datasets the paper takes from the library (Industry, MKT/SMB/HML, FF-1-factor, FF-4-factor), 1963 to November 2004 with a 120-month estimation window, so July 1973 to November 2004 out of sample, and runs the same rules forward to August 2026.

Rebalancing. Monthly. The optimiser forms each month's weights from the previous month's weights after the month's returns have moved them, under the constraint set in force. The turnover cap of 2 per cent one-way a month is relaxed to the smallest feasible turnover in the months in which the benchmark itself moves more than the cap allows, and those months are counted.

What is known at each date. The covariance comes from the window before the month (120 monthly returns, or three years of daily returns), the industry betas from the previous three years of daily returns, and the calibration of a forecast from the same path's previous 60 months. Nothing from the month itself or later enters a weight. French's files are a single vintage, so every month's returns are as French had revised them at the download date, which a portfolio run in real time would not have had.

Costs. Returns are gross of fees. Trading cost is charged at 50 basis points per unit of one-way turnover where stated (notebook 12 also charges 100). The benchmark itself turns over 0.39 per cent a month, which costs an index fund 2.3 basis points a year at the same rate.

Version 2, module A. The factor objective's portfolio is the one that maximises the sum of its active exposures to the four targeted factors, each in the direction of its premium and weighted equally in beta units, under every constraint of the mandate and a budget on its forecast tracking error written as a second-order cone, so the problem is a cone program where version 1's was a quadratic program. The betas are those of the three years of daily returns before the month, as in the attribution, and the forecast comes from the same covariance estimate as in version 1. A month in which the mandate's tracking-error-minimising portfolio already exceeds the budget takes that portfolio and is counted. Every expectation was written before any path ran, two of them restated from the arithmetic of notebook 14 before the rolling run with the earlier levels kept and marked, and the four verdicts not met are reported in the same words as the three met, each with its arithmetic. The exposure bought per factor and the gap per unit of exposure bought are reported with no expectation attached.

What the study cannot show. It is a simulation on industry portfolios, so a stock-level mandate may behave differently. The active returns are within one standard error of zero on every path of version 1 and within two on every path of version 2, so the tilt's cost in return is indistinguishable from zero, the factor objective's return is too, and no claim about skill or a premium follows. The forecasts are those of five covariance estimators on one data set, and the tracking errors realised here are what those estimators and this mandate produced over these 47 years. The write-up is the README and the two notes in the repository, where every number comes from the tables under results/202608/.

Terms

1/N
the rule that holds every asset at the same weight and rebalances to it each month.
rule
any of the nine portfolio rules of the replication: 1/N, sample-based mean-variance, Bayes-Stein, minimum variance, the value-weighted market, and the short-sale-constrained versions of the three optimising rules, with the generalised minimum-variance rule.
Sharpe ratio
the mean monthly excess return divided by its standard deviation. An excess return is a return minus the one-month Treasury bill rate.
turnover
the sum of absolute weight changes at a rebalance. One-way turnover is half of it.
band
an interval, fixed before the code ran, within which a replicated number counts as matching the published one. A cell a band applies to is gated and receives a verdict, pass or miss.
benchmark
the index the portfolio tracks, the cap-weighted (weighted by market capitalisation) combination of the 49 industries.
tracking error
the standard deviation of the monthly difference between the portfolio's return and the benchmark's, times the square root of 12. The forecast is the optimiser's own, the realised the measured one.
basis point
a hundredth of a percentage point.
covariance estimator
a method that turns a window of returns into a covariance matrix, the table of the assets' variances and covariances: the sample covariance, Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage towards a scaled identity, a six-factor model, a principal-component model. The robust portfolio minimises the worst forecast across the five estimates, and RiskMetrics is an exponentially weighted sample covariance.
minimum-variance portfolio
the portfolio with the smallest forecast variance under its constraints.
constraint set
one of the cumulative sets C0 to C3: long-only, plus an active weight bound of 2 percentage points per industry, plus a one-way turnover cap of 2 per cent a month, plus the mandate (the tilt, the exclusions of tobacco and weapons and a beta of one to the benchmark). The tilt alone, C2 plus the tilt, runs beside them as a sensitivity and is the fourth step of figure 3.
bias statistic
the standard deviation of the monthly active return divided by the same month's forecast (the forecast tracking error over the square root of twelve). It is 1 for a calibrated forecast and above 1 for an under-forecast. Figure 2 shows it over the whole period and by decade, for the forecast as made and for the calibrated forecast.
active return
the portfolio's return minus the benchmark's. The active weight of an industry is its portfolio weight minus its benchmark weight.
factor
a return series that many assets move with: the market (Mkt-RF), size (SMB), value (HML), profitability (RMW), investment (CMA) and momentum (Mom) factors of Fama and French and Carhart.
active exposure
the sum over industries of the active weight times the industry's beta (the slope of its return on the factor's) to the factor.
attribution
the split of a month's active return into the six contributions (exposure times the factor's return) and a residual. The factor share is one minus the variance of the residual over the variance of the active return.
standard error
the standard deviation of an estimate. For a mean active return over 47 years, it is the realised tracking error over the square root of 47.
vintage
the version of French's files on the download date, named by the CRSP cut French built them from.
excess return
a return minus the one-month Treasury bill rate.
out-of-sample
said of a return earned in the month after the estimation window, the 120 months a rule sees when it forms its weights.
gated
said of a cell a band applies to.
verdict
pass or miss for a gated cell. A cell with no band is reported beside the published number and has no verdict.
Bayes-Stein
the rule of Jorion (1986) that shrinks each estimated mean towards one common value.
shrinkage
pulling an estimate towards a simpler target.
beta
the slope of an asset's return on a factor's return, or on the benchmark's.
active weight
the portfolio weight minus the benchmark weight.
market capitalisation
the number of a firm's shares times their price.
cap-weighted
weighted by market capitalisation.
value-weighted
said of French's industry returns: each firm counts in proportion to its market capitalisation at the start of the month.
turnover cap
an upper limit on one-way turnover per rebalance, 2 per cent a month in the extension.
tilt
a constraint holding named industries below their benchmark weight, here Coal, Oil and Utilities at no more than half of it.
mandate
the whole set of rules the extension's portfolio obeys: the tilt, the exclusion of tobacco and weapons, and a beta of one to the benchmark, within the active weight bound and the turnover cap.
residual
the active return minus the six factor contributions, the part the industries' own returns account for.
contribution
the part of a month's active return one factor accounts for, the active exposure to it times its return in the month.
estimation window
the months of returns a rule sees when it forms its weights, 120 in the paper.
path
one covariance estimator run under one constraint set month by month over the 566 months. In version 2, one estimator at one budget with one set of targeted factors.
block bootstrap
resampling the months in blocks of twelve, so that the months of a block keep their order and their dependence, and recomputing a statistic on each resample. The band is the interval between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles across 2,000 resamples.
factor objective
in version 2, the quantity the optimiser maximises: the sum of the portfolio's active exposures to the targeted factors, each counted in the direction of its premium. It is not a tilt, which keeps its one meaning, the industry reduction.
targeted factors
the four of the six factors with a documented premium: value, profitability, investment and momentum, each in the direction of its premium, weighted equally in beta units. The premium is the factor's average return over long samples as the literature reports it, and the study estimates none.
budget
the limit on the forecast tracking error of the factor objective's portfolio, 75, 100 or 150 basis points a year. A month in which the mandate's tracking-error-minimising portfolio already exceeds it takes that portfolio and is counted.
main path
one of the fifteen paths of the five covariance estimators at the three budgets. The robust, RiskMetrics and single-factor paths at 100 basis points are sensitivities.
targeted total
the sum of the four targeted factors' contributions to a path's active return, per year.
factor share
the share of a path's active return's variance that the six contributions explain, one minus the variance of the residual over the variance of the active return. The active return's variance splits into the factor total's own variance, the residual's and twice their covariance, which is negative when the two parts move against each other.
exposure bought
a path's mean active exposure per basis point of the tracking error it realised. Per factor, the mean active exposure to that factor over the realised tracking error in basis points a year.
gap
for a month, the factor objective chosen with the betas estimated before the month minus the value the same active weights carry with the betas estimated after it. A path's gap is the mean absolute value over its months, and the gap per unit is that mean over the path's mean factor objective, the fraction of the exposure bought that the next month's betas take back.
cone program
an optimisation with a constraint written as a cone, which the solver reaches to a set precision. A quadratic program, whose objective is quadratic and whose constraints are linear, the solver reaches exactly. The budget makes the factor objective's problem a cone program.
