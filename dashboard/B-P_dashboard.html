  • Both naive portfolios earned more active return over the sample: before costs the naive tilt +13.5 basis points a year against +1.8 to +6.9, and the tilted index +16.4 against +2.3 to +15.5. No test of that difference was run. The gap has two sources: the spread between the other industries' return and that of the three tilted ones, which the naive tilt holds and the optimised portfolios partly hedge, and, after costs, the naive portfolios' lower turnover.
  • The replication matches the paper: 29 of the 30 Sharpe ratios tested are within 0.03 of the published values, and all 30 turnover figures tested are within 25 per cent. In the 261 months since the paper, no optimising rule's Sharpe ratio exceeds 1/N's by a margin that is statistically significant at the 5 per cent level.

Whether the paper's Sharpe ratios and turnover figures reproduce, and how the rules have done since.

DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009) asked whether optimised portfolios beat 1/N out of sample and found that none of their optimising rules did so reliably. Their rules include sample mean-variance, Bayes-Stein, which shrinks the estimated means towards a common value, and minimum variance, the portfolio with the smallest estimated variance. Their Sharpe ratios and turnover figures are recomputed here on four of the five datasets the paper takes from French's library, each compared with the published value within a set margin, and the comparison is run forward to August 2026.

How much tracking error the optimiser saves under the tilt, and at what turnover.

An index fund that holds Coal, Oil and Utilities at no more than half their benchmark weight has to put the weight it removes somewhere. The optimised portfolios put it where an optimiser says. Six of them each use one covariance estimator (a method that turns a window of past returns into a table of variances and covariances), and the seventh is a robust portfolio built from five of the six. All seven hold every other industry within 2 percentage points of its benchmark weight and trade at most 2 per cent a month. The naive tilt holds the three at exactly half and spreads the removed weight over the other 46 industries in proportion to their benchmark weights, with no risk model. Both start as the benchmark in July 1979 and rebalance every month.

The data, what is known at each date, the costs, what the study cannot show, and the terms.

Method

Universe and period. Parts 2 and 3 run on Kenneth R. French's 49 value-weighted industry portfolios and his six factors, downloaded at run time, vintage 202608, over the 566 months from July 1979 to August 2026. The benchmark is the cap-weighted combination of the 49, rebuilt from French's firm counts and average sizes. The replication uses four of the five datasets the paper takes from the library (Industry, MKT/SMB/HML, FF-1-factor, FF-4-factor), with a 120-month estimation window, so July 1973 to November 2004 out of sample, and then every month to August 2026.

The optimised portfolios. They come from the companion study, Which risk model for an index mandate?, whose tables and code are copied unchanged into this repository from its commit ddbb75c. Each month the optimiser forms the long-only weights with the smallest forecast tracking error under the tilt, or the largest factor objective within the budget, with one of six covariance estimators (methods that turn a window of past returns into a table of variances and covariances): the sample covariance, Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage, a six-factor model and a principal-component model on three years of daily returns, the sample covariance of 120 monthly returns, and RiskMetrics on five years of daily returns. The seventh optimised portfolio is a robust portfolio built from the first five estimators at once. Before any figure of a naive portfolio is computed, one optimised portfolio in each part is rebuilt from the copied code and checked against its recorded path.

The two naive portfolios. The naive tilt halves Coal, Oil and Utilities and scales the other 46 industries up by the same multiple. The tilted index excludes tobacco and weapons as well, then multiplies each of the other 44 weights by one plus k times the distance of the industry's factor score from their weighted average score, with k the largest value the 2-point limit and the floor at zero allow. Neither is held to the turnover limit, and the tilted index is not held to a beta of one.

What is known at each date. Every weight is formed from data before its month: the covariance estimates from the window before the month and the industries' factor exposures from the three years of daily returns before it. French's files are a single vintage, so every month's returns are as French had revised them at the download date, which a portfolio run in real time would not have had.

Costs. Returns are gross of fees. Trading cost is charged at 50 or 100 basis points per unit of one-way turnover where stated, after the fact.

Tests. Under the tilt, each pair of realised tracking errors is compared with the paired variance test of Ledoit and Wolf (2011), resampling blocks of 12 months 2,000 times. Under the factor objective, each difference in exposure per basis point is computed on 2,000 block resamples of the same months for both portfolios. Holm's correction is applied across the seven comparisons of each part. The returns carry no test. The margins of the replication were fixed before the code ran, except the limit on which Sharpe ratios are tested, set after the first replication results. The notebooks mark the two checks made after the results were known: the differences in information ratio beside their standard errors, and the split of the naive tilt's active return into the spread.

What the study cannot show. Each naive portfolio is one construction, and the tilted index's exposure depends on its form, since its largest industries set k for all the others. The assets are industry portfolios, so a stock-level mandate may behave differently. The comparison of returns rests on one sample, and the spread behind the naive tilt's return averaged +1.22 per cent a year with a standard deviation of 15.28.

Terms

1/N
the rule that holds every asset at the same weight and rebalances to it each month.
Rule
any of the nine portfolio rules of the replication: 1/N, sample-based mean-variance, Bayes-Stein, minimum variance, the value-weighted market, the short-sale-constrained versions of the three optimising rules, and the generalised minimum-variance rule.
Bayes-Stein
the rule of Jorion (1986) that shrinks each estimated mean towards one common value.
Estimation window
the months of returns a rule sees when it forms its weights, 120 in the paper.
Out-of-sample
the months after a rule's estimation window, on which its weights are tested.
Sharpe ratio
the mean monthly excess return, i.e., the return minus the one-month Treasury bill rate, divided by its standard deviation.
Turnover
the sum of absolute weight changes at a rebalance. One-way turnover is half of it, the share of the portfolio sold.
Vintage
the version of French's files, named by the CRSP database they were built from.
Jobson-Korkie test
the test of whether two Sharpe ratios differ, with Memmel's correction, as the paper uses it.
Cap-weighted
weighted by market capitalisation, i.e., each company's share price times its shares outstanding.
Value-weighted
the same as cap-weighted, French's word for his portfolios.
Benchmark
the index the portfolio tracks, the cap-weighted combination of the 49 industries.
Tracking error
the standard deviation of the monthly difference between the portfolio's return and the benchmark's, times the square root of 12. It is forecast before the month and realised after it.
Active weight
an industry's weight in the portfolio minus its benchmark weight.
Active return
the portfolio's return minus the benchmark's.
Basis point
a hundredth of a percentage point.
Tilt
holding Coal, Oil and Utilities at no more than half their benchmark weight.
Covariance estimate
the table of the variances and covariances of the industries' returns, from which the optimiser forecasts tracking error.
Optimised portfolio
the portfolio the optimiser holds month by month with one of the six covariance estimators, or as the robust portfolio built from five of them.
Naive tilt
the index with Coal, Oil and Utilities held at half their index weight and the removed weight spread over the other 46 industries in proportion to their index weights, rebalanced to that target every month, with no risk model.
Tilted index
the index under the mandate's tilt and exclusions, with each of the other 44 industries' weights changed by a percentage proportional to the distance of its factor score from their weighted average score, as far as the 2-point limit and a floor of zero allow, rebalanced every month, with no risk model.
The two naive portfolios
the naive tilt and the tilted index together.
Factor
a return series that many assets move with: the market (Mkt-RF), size (SMB), value (HML), profitability (RMW), investment (CMA) and momentum (Mom) factors of Fama and French and Carhart.
Active exposure
the sum over industries of the active weight times the industry's beta to the factor, i.e., the slope of its return on the factor's.
Factor score
the sum of an industry's exposures to value, profitability, investment and momentum.
Factor objective
the sum of the portfolio's active exposures to value, profitability, investment and momentum.
Budget
the limit on the forecast tracking error of the optimiser's portfolio under the factor objective, 100 basis points a year in the main comparison.
Exposure per basis point
the mean factor objective divided by the realised tracking error in basis points a year.
Information ratio
the active return per year divided by the realised tracking error.
Attribution
the split of a month's active return into six contributions, each the active exposure to a factor times the factor's return, and a residual.
Residual
the part of the active return the six factors do not explain, i.e., the active return minus the six contributions.
Standard error
the standard deviation an estimate would show across repeated samples of the same length.
Paired variance test
the test of Ledoit and Wolf (2011) of whether two portfolios, measured on the same months, have the same tracking error.
Block bootstrap
resampling the months in blocks of twelve, so that the months of a block keep their order, and recomputing a statistic on each of 2,000 resamples.
Holm's correction
an adjustment of p-values for the number of comparisons read together (Holm, 1979).
Spread
the cap-weighted return of the 46 industries outside the tilt minus that of Coal, Oil and Utilities.
